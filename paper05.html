"Ownership, Not Just Happy Talk": Co-Designing a Participatory Large Language Model for Journalism

Abstract
English

Journalism has emerged as an essential domain for understanding the uses, limitations, and impacts of large language models (LLMs) in the workplace. News organizations face divergent financial incentives: LLMs already permeate newswork processes within financially constrained organizations, even as ongoing legal challenges assert that AI companies violate their copyright. At stake are key questions about what LLMs are created to do, and by whom: How might a journalist-led LLM work, and what can participatory design illuminate about the present-day challenges about adapting "one-size-fits-all" foundation models to a given context of use? In this paper, we undertake a co-design exploration to understand how a participatory approach to LLMs might address opportunities and challenges around AI in journalism. Our 20 interviews with reporters, data journalists, editors, labor organizers, product leads, and executives highlight macro, meso, and micro tensions that designing for this opportunity space must address. From these desiderata, we describe the result of our co-design work: organizational structures and functionality for a journalist-controlled LLM. In closing, we discuss the limitations of commercial foundation models for workplace use, and the methodological implications of applying participatory methods to LLM co-design.

1. Introduction · 引言
English

The tenuous relationship between journalism and technological innovation has reached a new inflection point. Digitization, datafication, and platformization have transformed how journalists produce and audiences consume news, and affected the types and overall quality of news reporting. As ad revenues shrink, the news industry faces a monetization crisis: more than one third of journalists reported layoffs or buyouts at their organization in 2024. Even as the industry struggles against these headwinds, sustainable news ecosystems remain vital for healthy community attachment, toward democratic participation, and to hold powers to account.

Widespread adoption of generative AI and large language models (LLMs) has only introduced new crises for journalists and news consumers. Today's advanced models rely on data scraped from news outlets. Simultaneously, those very models threaten journalists' livelihoods by automating fundamental aspects of their craft in an industry already grappling with financial instability. While language technologies have long been used for template-ready forms of news production, like financial and sports briefings, the newest language technologies have broadened the scope of potential automated coverage. Already today, more than 80% of newsrooms are estimated to leverage AI for newswork.

These challenges have led to divergent opinions and approaches to generative AI across journalism. Much of the industry is optimistic that AI could boost revenues toward stabilizing journalism's economic crisis, by personalizing news products to audiences, increasing content reach, and optimizing workflow efficiency. Still, many journalists take issue with how tech companies have broadly profited from data scraped from news outlets without sharing revenues back to newsrooms. These tensions have led newsrooms to take concrete—and divergent—actions. Some newsrooms have struck deals with tech companies like OpenAI, hoping to recoup their intellectual property. Others have taken legal action against OpenAI and Microsoft, citing copyright infringement.

In light of the increasing corporate capture of data and AI tools across the news industry, we argue that there is a critical opportunity to instead pursue a participatory approach to the development and governance of LLMs and AI, owned and led by journalists. The FAccT community has broadly explored how participatory approaches to AI might ensure that key stakeholders, data providers, and affected communities maintain decision-making power in the design of AI systems. In the present moment, where journalists' work is expropriated by corporate LLMs without compensation or design input, questions of meaningful participation are particularly salient.

With this motivation, in this work, we set out to conduct a participatory approach to building an LLM, with, for, and led by journalists. We began by establishing a practitioner-researcher partnership, including practitioners with deep experience as journalists and researchers from the FAccT and HCI communities steeped in participatory approaches. Our team then conducted a study with 20 journalists across multiple locations, organization sizes, and roles within newsmaking. Through these interviews, we synthesized key tensions that must be addressed by a journalist-controlled LLM to be successful: structural and market forces, inter-organization competition, and effects on individual journalists and newsrooms. Responding to these tensions and as part of a co-design exercise with participants, we then present an iterated proposal for how to structure a journalist-controlled LLM. We close with reflections on the implications of our findings for commercial foundation models and methodological lessons for FAccT scholars and practitioners similarly interested in helping affected workforces and communities shape these novel technologies.

2. Related Works · 相关工作
English

Our work sits at the intersection between AI's effect on journalism (2.1) and participatory AI (2.2).

2.1 AI in Journalism. Generative AI has sprawled in influence across the news media ecosystem. The ability to produce naturalistic and inexpensive text, images, and videos has inspired imagination across the industry. An estimated 82% of newsrooms use AI today, up from 37% in 2019, with the leading use cases being the automation of news writing, data analysis, and personalization. LLMs have impacted not only how journalists produce news, but also how people consume media and information. Audiences increasingly rely on AI-augmented services to access news content, e.g., through news aggregation platforms like Apple News. Search platforms, too, now use LLMs to supply information: Google, Perplexity, and other search engines now answer queries with summaries generated from news content.

The rapid uptake of these tools has not been without concern. Practitioners and scholars have raised that AI might exacerbate issues of credibility and authenticity between journalists and their audiences, especially as AI's problems of bias, impact on privacy, and wider ethical implications remain unresolved. Cools and Diakopoulos synthesized a set of "perils" that loom (lack of judgment, hallucination and biases, and loss of autonomy), even as journalists explore attendant "possibilities" (efficiency and scale, increased personalization).

Uptake of automation has historically been slow among journalists: changes in workflow can disrupt deadlines and breaking news, and their training in teasing out complicated narratives makes them slower to adopt new technologies. Against this historical hesitancy, recent uses of AI in news have become cautionary tales. In 2023, Sports Illustrated outsourced content creation to a third-party vendor that used generative AI to create fake author biographies and photos, inciting debate around the lack of transparency in AI news reporting. Similarly, Apple News recently withdrew a feature that automatically summarized news stories after its LLM generated false information.

Beyond these impacts on public trust, scholars have also expressed concerns around what AI means for journalistic work. Already, journalists perceive generative AI as a greater threat, with a steeper learning curve, than prior types of automated news writing. Jones et al. found journalists increasingly rely on AI tools, but do not find these systems "intelligible." Xiao et al. found journalists perceived AI as particularly "inaccessible," and argued for more collaboration with technology companies when out-of-the-box AI tools do not meet journalistic needs. Dodds et al. identified knowledge silos within news organizations: how the knowledge to understand AI—technical know-how, strategy, and ethical alignment—is currently siloed across roles, teams, and levels of seniority.

This paper complements this literature by taking a participatory approach to developing LLMs for journalists that centers organizational needs and decision-making, preceding human-AI interfaces. We argue that considering organizational needs and constraints (at both inter- and intra-organizational scales) is key to bridging the gap between what is technically feasible, what is organizationally realistic, and what is socially responsible.

2.2 Participatory Approaches to LLMs. To address the perils and possibilities of AI in journalism, we adopt what FAccT has framed as a participatory approach to AI. This approach recognizes first that the development of AI models, as well as the compilation and storage of the datasets they are trained on, is heavily centralized within large corporations. Downstream applications and users must accept a pre-trained model as-is, without control over or transparency into data sources, underlying design decisions, fine-tuning or alignment procedures, and future data or model changes.

Participatory approaches stand as a potential antidote to these imbalances: to distribute power to those who typically do not have it. Scholars have characterized a spectrum of approaches, from those that are more tokenistic (e.g., one-time user feedback that shapes model fine-tuning) to those that attempt to meaningfully share decision-making power (e.g., sustained relationships with affected communities who are involved in many project stages, from data collection to evaluation). The problem of how to meaningfully share power is particularly challenging for today's LLMs, due to their centralized and largely corporate control, and the scale of resources devoted to their commercialization as "one-size-fits-all" technologies. Our work builds on Suresh et al.'s call to do this for journalism, with journalists, drawing out lessons not only for journalism as a domain for AI application, but also participatory design and governance of AI.

参与式方法可以成为这些不平衡的潜在解药：将权力分配给通常不拥有权力的人。学者们描述了多种方法的光谱，从更具象征性的方法（如一次性用户反馈来影响模型微调）到试图有意义地分享决策权的方法（如与受影响社区建立持续关系，使他们参与从数据收集到评估的多个项目阶段）。对于当今的LLM而言，如何有意义地分享权力的问题尤其具有挑战性，因为这些模型集中且主要由企业控制，并且有大量资源投入其作为"一刀切"技术的商业化。我们的工作建立在Suresh等人将参与式方法应用于新闻业的呼吁之上，不仅为新闻业作为AI应用领域提炼经验，也为AI的参与式设计和治理提供启示。

3. Methods · 研究方法
English

Our work took a participatory approach based on (i) participatory action research, (ii) semi-structured interviews, and (iii) co-design. In AI and technology design, participatory methods offer principles and practices for researchers to more fully collaborate with communities impacted by technology, and to better reflect their values, preferences, and needs in resulting systems. Our methods were approved by a university Institutional Review Board (IRB).

3.1 Participatory Action Research (PAR). PAR is an approach to embedding research in a community's real-world needs and concerns. To begin, a research team forges a close collaboration with a person or organization in a field of interest (the field partner). Throughout a study, the research team centers the questions most salient to the field partner, analyzes data with the partner, and works with the partner to decide next steps: an iterative and collaborative process known as the "action-reflection cycle."

Our team first met our field partner, a senior product manager working for a national news organization, for a conversation about how newsrooms can better assert intellectual property protections against data scraping from AI companies. Through these conversations, we noticed overlap in our goals: the researchers wanted to think through this complex set of problems as a case study in participatory AI, and our field partner wanted to refine institutional responses that could help the news industry navigate these challenges.

Our research goals evolved through the action-reflection cycle. Initially, we planned to create a working demo of a journalist-led LLM using newsroom partners' data. As we worked toward the prototype, we realized more research was needed to understand the macroeconomic factors constraining and incentivizing newsroom investment in AI, the inter-organizational considerations shaping how newsrooms regarded each other, and user-level opportunities and challenges for LLM tools. With our field partner, we set the following research questions: (RQ1 Macro) What are the market and structural factors that shape newsroom decision-making about AI? (RQ2 Meso) What are the organizational and technical requirements needed to enable the creation of a participatory design-created, newsroom-owned, and journalism-specific LLM? (RQ3 Micro) What new functionality might a participatory LLM enable in a journalist's day-to-day—and what material and affective demands would participation levy upon them in return?

3.2 Semi-structured Interviews. We conducted 20 semi-structured interviews with reporters, data journalists, editors, labor organizers, product leads, and executives of news organizations, as well as leaders of nonprofits dedicated supporting news organizations. Our sampling strategy aimed to speak with those from both large organizations and small; online-only, newspapers, radio, and TV broadcast; varied ownership and revenue models; and different relationships to AI companies. Participants were recruited via email outreach to relevant professional networks. Interviews were 60 minutes long, and all participants were offered $50 Visa gift cards for completed interviews. Interviews were conducted online over Zoom and recorded for transcription.

Each interview had two parts. First, a descriptive section, which set out to understand the person's role and responsibilities, and how they currently used AI, if at all. Questions probed the participant's perspective on newsroom readiness to contribute data toward a hypothetical industry-controlled LLM: for example, participants were asked to describe how much of their archive is digitized, and in what formats.

3.3 Participatory Design Methods. Part two of each interview drew on participatory design fiction, a form of artifact-based interviewing. In this method, researchers use artifacts (like low- or medium-fidelity technology prototypes or hypothetical specifications) as probes for eliciting knowledge from users that is otherwise challenging to access directly or in the abstract, such as ethical values and contextual considerations. To elicit the value proposition and organizational constraints that would shape their interest in a participatory LLM, we created a PDF inviting participants to join a would-be organization called the "Newsroom Tooling Alliance." Our probe included an invitation email and a 1-page proposal of an inter-organizational collaboration to create a journalism-specific LLM.

Participants were reminded that the invitation was not from an actual organization, but instead was a way to understand key contours of the context that such an initiative must address. Interviewers encouraged participants to be honest about the limits and fundamental barriers of the artifact. Following seminal work on navigating participant response bias in HCI, we were also attentive to power dynamics in this exchange. When interviewing more precarious workers, such as frontline reporters or editors, interviewers emphasized the value of their expertise and the team's interest in their views on the limits and problems with the proposal.

3.4 Data Analysis. All interviews were recorded on Zoom and transcribed by Trint and Otter AI. Transcripts were cross-checked by a human researcher for accuracy. Data was analyzed using an iterative inductive qualitative coding approach based on grounded theory. Our initial code categories corresponded to our research questions: macro, for market-level factors shaping newsroom decision-making about AI; meso, for inter-organizational factors shaping newsrooms' decisions to partake in LLMs; and micro, for user-level needs and functionality that a journalism-specific LLM would enable. Through numerous discussions as a research team, we made iterative passes through the data to surface sub-categories in each of these areas, which we consolidated through additional passes to arrive at a final set of codes.

Our final set of codes and coded data resulted in desiderata for a participant-led LLM. However, some of our findings could not be fully resolved at the level of design specifications: instead, we came to understand them as "constitutive tensions": key frictions underlying the creation of a participatory LLM for journalism to which our own design must respond.

我们的工作采用了基于（i）参与式行动研究、（ii）半结构化访谈和（iii）协同设计的参与式方法。在AI和技术设计中，参与式方法为研究者提供了与受技术影响的社区更充分合作的原则和实践，并更好地在最终系统中反映他们的价值观、偏好和需求。我们的方法已获大学机构审查委员会（IRB）批准。

3.1 参与式行动研究（PAR）。PAR是一种将研究嵌入社区真实需求和关注点的方法。首先，研究团队与感兴趣领域中的个人或组织（现场合作伙伴）建立密切合作。在研究中，研究团队以现场合作伙伴最关心的问题为中心，与合作伙伴一起分析数据，并共同决定下一步行动：这是一个被称为"行动-反思循环"的迭代协作过程。

我们的团队首先与我们的现场合作伙伴——一位服务于全国性新闻组织的高级产品经理——会面，讨论新闻编辑室如何更好地维护知识产权免受AI公司的数据抓取。通过这些对话，我们注意到目标的重叠：研究者希望将这一系列复杂问题作为参与式AI的案例研究进行思考，而我们的现场合作伙伴希望完善能够帮助新闻行业应对这些挑战的制度性应对措施。

我们的研究目标通过行动-反思循环逐步演变。最初，我们计划使用新闻编辑室合作伙伴的数据创建一个记者主导的LLM工作演示。在开发原型的过程中，我们意识到需要更多研究来理解制约和激励新闻编辑室投资AI的宏观经济因素、影响新闻编辑室相互看待方式的组织间考量、以及LLM工具在用户层面的机遇和挑战。我们与现场合作伙伴共同确定了以下研究问题：（RQ1 宏观）塑造新闻编辑室AI决策的市场和结构因素是什么？（RQ2 中观）创建一个参与式设计的、新闻编辑室拥有的、特定于新闻业的LLM需要哪些组织和技术要求？（RQ3 微观）参与式LLM可以在记者的日常工作中实现哪些新功能？参与又会给他们带来怎样的物质和情感要求？

3.2 半结构化访谈。我们对记者、数据新闻工作者、编辑、劳工组织者、产品负责人和新闻机构高管以及致力于支持新闻组织的非营利组织领导者进行了20次半结构化访谈。我们的抽样策略旨在涵盖大型和小型组织、纯在线媒体、报纸、广播和电视广播、不同的所有权和收入模式、以及与AI公司不同的关系。参与者通过电子邮件联系相关专业网络进行招募。每次访谈时长60分钟，所有完成访谈的参与者均获得50美元Visa礼品卡。访谈通过Zoom在线进行并录音以供转录。

每次访谈分两部分进行。首先是描述性部分，旨在了解该人的角色和职责，以及他们目前如何使用AI（如果有的话）。问题探讨了参与者对新闻编辑室是否准备好为一个假设的行业控制的LLM贡献数据的看法：例如，参与者被要求描述他们的档案中有多少已经数字化，以及采用何种格式。

3.3 参与式设计方法。每次访谈的第二部分借鉴了参与式设计虚构（participatory design fiction），一种基于人工制品的访谈形式。在这种方法中，研究者使用人工制品（如低或中保真技术原型或假设性规格说明）作为探针，引出用户难以直接或抽象获取的知识，如伦理价值和情境考量。为了引出影响他们对参与式LLM兴趣的价值主张和组织约束，我们创建了一个PDF，邀请参与者加入一个名为"新闻编辑室工具联盟"（NTA）的拟议组织。我们的探针包括一封邀请邮件和一页关于创建新闻业特定LLM的组织间协作提案。

参与者被告知，邀请并非来自实际组织，而是一种理解此类倡议必须解决的关键背景轮廓的方式。访谈者鼓励参与者坦诚对待人工制品的局限性和根本障碍。借鉴人机交互中处理参与者反应偏差的开创性工作，我们同样关注这一互动中的权力动态。在采访更不稳定的工作者（如一线记者或编辑）时，访谈者强调其专业知识价值以及团队对他们关于提案局限性和问题意见的兴趣。

3.4 数据分析。所有访谈均在Zoom上录制，并由Trint和Otter AI转录。转录文本由一位人类研究者交叉核对以确保准确性。数据采用基于扎根理论的迭代归纳定性编码方法进行分析。我们的初始编码类别对应我们的研究问题：宏观层面，对应影响新闻编辑室AI决策的市场因素；中观层面，对应影响新闻编辑室参与LLM决策的组织间因素；微观层面，对应新闻业特定LLM将支持的用户级需求和功能。通过研究团队的多次讨论，我们对数据进行迭代式的通读，以揭示这些领域中的子类别，并通过额外的通读进行整合，最终形成一套编码。

我们的最终编码和编码数据产生了参与者主导LLM的需求规格。然而，我们的一些发现无法在设计规格层面完全解决：相反，我们将其理解为"构成性张力"——即创建新闻业参与式LLM所面临的关键摩擦，而我们的设计必须回应这些张力。

4. Tensions — Macro Level · 张力分析——宏观层面
English

Our interviews and design methods revealed a complex set of tensions at the macro, meso, and micro levels that shape journalism as a design space for a participatory AI.

4.1 Macro Tensions. Interviewees described intense pressures at the level of market dynamics, professional culture, and strategy. Participants described tensions around: (i) the push to create more content versus the reality of diminishing resources; (ii) the need to follow audience changes in how they consume information versus industry loss of control; (iii) the need for agility and novel competencies versus the traditional culture and norms of the profession; and (iv) the benefits of partnering with existing technology companies versus concerns of just compensation and fairness. Many described these centrifugal forces as pulling the industry in different directions, both toward and away from using AI, thus eliciting ambivalent or "competing thoughts" for those on-the-ground.

4.1.1 The push to create more content versus diminishing resources. Participants reported tremendous pressure on newsrooms to make more content with diminishing resources. Many noted industry-wide nosedives in audiences and revenues, even as public appetite for content skyrockets. Local newsrooms struggle to make payroll and operate at a constant and acute risk of closing. Even as income streams fall, many of the costs to reporting and verifying information remain high. The industry was described as "bleeding money." These quotes reinforce the hard truth that reporting news is inherently resource-intensive, due to the work needed to collect novel information and fact-check it for accuracy.

4.1.2 Following audience changes versus industry loss of control. Part of the margin-tightening is due to the industry losing its share of its audience, who are shifting news consumption habits more readily than the industry has adapted its business models. Several participants analogized the present shifts with AI companies to past experiences with social media platforms, during which newsrooms were subject to capricious changes in how newsfeed algorithms worked—dramatically changing news industry revenues overnight. As P13 recalled: "Facebook gave [newsrooms] a ton of traffic for free for a long time and then suddenly changed direction. It is very possible that OpenAI and these other platforms will do the same thing." These past experiences underscored an overall desire for greater industry self-determination, including payment structures that benefit newsrooms and restore their control.

4.1.3 Agility and novel competencies versus traditional culture. Many participants noted that the technical competencies and appetite for innovation needed to adopt AI contrasted sharply against the timeworn traditional culture of the journalistic profession. One respondent described the past cultural norm as "Don't offend anybody, don't break anything"—innovation was punished. While larger news conglomerates have technology roles, the long tail of smaller newsrooms often lacked in-house technical personnel and resources to "to fully tap into [new technologies] and don't even know where to get started." At the same time, all respondents acknowledged the need for the industry to adapt: "AI is inevitable, so it makes sense to get ahead of it or to use it in a way that is not extractive to the industry."

4.1.4 Partnering with tech companies versus just compensation. Respondents described the industry as facing a difficult decision. On the one hand, they noted many benefits of relying on AI companies' payments through licensing agreements. On the other, many described a deep sense of offense at how AI companies have been able to monetize work they did not do or pay for. As P05 said: "I'll be honest that it's also just my own strong sense of justice. I get really pissed off when people steal from us." Many participants expressed this sense of powerlessness. Part of participants' frustrations were due to larger newsrooms beginning to explore licensing agreements with OpenAI—News Corp, Condé Nast, Vox Media, and Axios have all signed agreements. Smaller newsrooms are being solicited primarily as users of commercial products rather than licensing partners. Interviewees who had concerns about corporate dominance were more supportive of a participatory, journalist-led LLM.

4.1.4 与科技公司合作vs公平报酬。受访者描述该行业面临着一个艰难的决定。一方面，他们注意到通过许可协议依赖AI公司支付的许多好处。另一方面，许多人对AI公司能够将他们未做或未付费的工作货币化深感不满。正如P05所说："老实说，这也是我自己强烈的正义感。当有人从我们这里偷东西时，我真的非常生气。"许多参与者表达了这种无力感。参与者沮丧的部分原因是较大的新闻编辑室开始探索与OpenAI的许可协议——新闻集团、康泰纳仕、Vox Media和Axios都已签署协议。较小的新闻编辑室主要被作为商业产品的用户而非许可合作伙伴来接触。对企业主导地位有担忧的受访者更支持参与式的、记者主导的LLM。

4.2 Meso Tensions · 中观张力
English

Given the structural tensions, how might news organizations at different sizes and localities cooperate toward a collectively owned LLM? We find that to create conditions for inter-organizational collaboration, a participatory LLM must address sensitivities around (i) mutual benefit versus competition with rivals, (ii) data pooling versus data protection, (iii) enabling versus constraining novel uses, and (iv) collective action versus local control.

4.2.1 Mutual benefit versus competition with rivals. Participants voiced enthusiasm for some form of cooperation between news organizations as part of an industry response to market pressures. However, participants at larger organizations noted barriers of "competitive defensiveness," where "everyone is trying to figure out how to do AI better than their immediate competitors." At the same time, many observed cooperation could help save the long tail of smaller and more local newsrooms by providing access to costly technical resources they would otherwise lack. As P16 noted, helping smaller publishers adapt to AI "would be incredibly exciting to anybody who watches the industry, and cares about democracy and the country and the world."

4.2.2 Data pooling versus data protection. Data rights were a particularly thorny flashpoint for tensions around cooperation and competition. Attuned to issues around ownership of news and copyright of journalistic work, many nevertheless saw benefits to sharing work product through data pooling toward a participatory LLM, especially for "stitching together geographies from a local perspective" (P16). Participants sought guarantees on how organizations or freelancers could get credit and proportional revenue share for contributions. Many identified a precedent in how journalists collaborate on investigative reporting by pooling documents. P01 recalled: "the general rule is, if you're worried that if someone else sees it, you're going to be in trouble, you just don't put it in any database." Time-worn social and technical protocols could be adapted to pool data for a participatory LLM.

4.2.3 Enabling versus constraining novel uses. Participants saw practical and philosophical tensions in how participating news organizations could agree on use cases. If one participating newsroom supported one use case from a jointly created participatory LLM, and another vehemently opposed it, how should the collective proceed? One axis of disagreement was the divide between maximizing journalists' efficiency and creating new and innovative uses. P03 noted: "You might have a well-equipped newsroom think about using [AI] in creative new ways, versus a newsroom that's vastly under-resourced and would rather spend time on shortcuts for rote tasks." Smaller and independent newsrooms were seen as leading the charge toward novel uses out of "real appetite, real innovation, and real openness," while larger newsrooms were likely to be stymied by institutional bureaucracy.

4.2.4 Collective action versus local control. Many participants said the entity who would lead a participatory LLM would have a large bearing on how it is received. The alliance would be maximally trustworthy if led by people with actual journalism experience. Questions included whether voting rights would be allocated evenly or in proportion to the size of member organizations; where freelancers would fit in; and how funding would influence the broader project. Crucially, the leadership would need to carefully manage the project's relationship to labor organizing, negotiating not only with management but also representatives of workers. Finally, the initiative would need to manage how member organizations worked with corporate tech actors. As P06 described: "[Is a participatory LLM project] going to have the funding to pay us what OpenAI would pay for that same data?" Maintaining long-term cooperation would require clear benefits and accountability structures.

4.3 Micro Tensions · 微观张力
English

Our research identified tensions in how a participatory LLM would influence the day-to-day work of individual journalists and editors. These tensions include (i) whether existing commercial LLMs are adequate versus the need for a participatory LLM, (ii) how to realize efficiency versus preserve the human element of newswork, and (iii) expanding versus exceeding or replacing existing labor capacity.

4.3.1 Commercial LLM adequacy versus the need for a participatory LLM. Participants listed many use cases that would make LLMs valuable to their workflows, many converging with needs assessed in previous work. Many were excited about the potential to speed and democratize access to data journalism, like projects to dig through municipal records or generate interactive charts. Other potential uses included support for rote tasks like data scraping and processing; optimizing headlines; flagging articles for fact-checking; searching archives; and supporting the reporting process. However, these ideas invited deeper questions about the fitness of existing commercial LLMs versus the additional benefits of a journalist-controlled LLM. While some participants already used commercial LLMs for tools like transcription and headline suggestions, many newsrooms have policies against sharing information with commercial models due to concerns about "leaking out our copyrighted material to models that are being trained on it" (P03)—indicating that while commercial models seem to meet needs, many newsrooms are wary of disclosing proprietary data.

4.3.2 Efficiency versus preserving the human element. Journalists described ways adapting LLMs could help them become more efficient, reducing burdens on their time. As P01 framed it: "How do we start to claw back the time so we can use the highest and best use of our brains to do the things that really matter?" Many wanted to recover time for the necessarily human aspects of journalism: sourcing, showing up to civic meetings, and being present in their communities. At the same time, interviewees expressed concerns about what can be lost when newsrooms come to rely on automation. The idea of using AI to fully automate content creation was particularly stirring. P10 said: "The spirit of the storytelling is not there if you're just using it to do everything for you." Participants' desire for human voices reflected an appreciation for complexity, contradiction, and human nuance—qualities that any newswriting pipeline must center.

4.3.2 Expanding versus replacing existing labor capacity. Respondents were amenable to how a participatory LLM could expand their knowledge and skills, even if this would create new job tasks. Many felt the incentives of a cooperative structure could overcome resistance to new work, and ultimately lower barriers to adoption: "we're not giving you a bunch of happy talk, we're actually giving you an ownership stake in the work that you're producing" (P09). However, participants had concerns about how automation could risk displacing their labor. Journalists are already very constrained in time and resources; the introduction of automation has a history of encouraging elimination of jobs instead of augmenting work. As P09 said: "There's two dominant emotions [around AI], suspicion and fear. You say this is R2-D2, but it's actually the Terminator." This concern was present even for use cases that seemed ripe for automation. Respondents agreed that any LLM, however participatory, would need to stick to use cases that supported journalists' work and be governed to avoid replacing them.

我们的研究识别了参与式LLM将如何影响个体记者和编辑日常工作的张力。这些张力包括：（i）现有商业LLM是否足够vs对参与式LLM的需求；（ii）如何实现效率vs保留新闻工作的人性元素；（iii）扩大vs超过或取代现有劳动力能力。

4.3.1 商业LLM的充分性vs参与式LLM的需求。参与者列举了许多使LLM对其工作流程有价值的用例，许多与先前工作中评估的需求相吻合。许多人对加速和民主化数据新闻获取——如挖掘市政记录或生成交互式图表的项目——的潜力感到兴奋。其他潜在用途包括支持数据抓取和处理等机械性任务；优化标题；标记需要事实核查的文章；搜索档案；以及支持报道过程。然而，这些想法引发了关于现有商业LLM的适用性与记者控制LLM额外好处之间更深层次的问题。虽然一些参与者已经在使用商业LLM进行转录和标题建议等工具，但许多新闻编辑室由于担心"将我们的版权材料泄露给正在利用这些材料进行训练的模型"而制定了禁止与商业模型共享信息的政策（P03）——这表明虽然商业模型似乎满足了需求，但许多新闻编辑室对披露专有数据持谨慎态度。

4.3.2 效率vs保留人性元素。记者描述了调整LLM以适应其工作流程如何帮助他们提高效率，减轻时间负担。正如P01所言："我们如何开始夺回时间，以便我们可以用我们大脑最高和最好的用途来做真正重要的事情？"许多人希望将时间回收用于新闻业必然的人性化方面：寻找来源、出席公民会议、在社区中保持存在。与此同时，受访者表达了对新闻编辑室依赖自动化可能失去什么的担忧。使用AI完全自动化内容创作的想法尤其令人不安。P10说："如果你只用它来做所有事情，讲故事的精神就不存在了。"参与者对人类声音的渴望反映了对复杂性、矛盾性和人类细微差别的欣赏——这是任何新闻写作流程都必须重视的品质。

4.3.3 扩大vs取代现有劳动力能力。受访者对参与式LLM可以扩展他们知识和技能的方式持开放态度，即使这会创造新的工作任务。许多人认为合作结构的激励可以克服对新工作的阻力，并最终降低采用技术的障碍："我们不是在给你们一堆空话，我们实际上在给你们所生产工作的所有权股份"（P09）。然而，参与者对自动化可能取代其劳动的风险表示担忧。记者在时间和资源上已经受到很大限制；引入自动化有着鼓励消除工作岗位而非增强工作的历史。正如P09所说："[围绕AI]有两种主导情绪，猜疑和恐惧。你说这是R2-D2，但它实际上是终结者。"这种担忧甚至存在于看起来适合自动化的用例中。受访者一致认为，任何LLM——无论多么参与式——都需要坚持支持记者工作的用例，并通过治理避免取代他们。

5. Co-Design Result: The Newsroom Tooling Alliance (NTA) · 协同设计成果：新闻编辑室工具联盟
English

Responding to the tensions described above, we present the results of our co-designed proposal for a journalism-specific LLM. First, a cooperative of member news organizations would work to create, steward, maintain, and promote a journalist-controlled LLM—what we have dubbed through our co-design work the Newsroom Tooling Alliance (NTA).

Structure. NTA members commit to contributing some of their work product to a collective pool of data, under terms governed by a common data use agreement. NTA would be responsible for supporting the costs associated with data intake through grant funds; there would be no cost of contribution to the members. Members have the right to include or exclude data at their discretion, e.g., to exclude reporters' notes but include their historical archives. The amount and quality of data contributed is used to prorate an individual member's revenue-share, to incentivize members to share more data. Data provenance is carefully traced to facilitate members' ability to remove their data from the pool.

Functionality. NTA staff data scientists would use the pooled data to fine-tune the most transparent and consentful open-source LLM available (or to experiment with creating a de novo LLM) in order to develop applications for journalistic tasks. One crucial piece of NTA membership is the ability and responsibility to vote on what use cases their data will be used for. NTA staff implement these applications and set up structures for members to help maintain them by evaluating their performance against holistic criteria—for example, auditing generated text for correctness, bias, and depth of coverage. This focus on participation in both design and governance ensures NTA products have a value-add over what could be developed without its journalist-led structure.

Governance. NTA members are billed for their usage of NTA tools at cost. Long-term, the NTA would also explore other revenue models, such as licensing access to newsroom data or releasing NTA products to non-members. One near-term priority would be to raise money for legal fees, in order to support members who might want to protect their intellectual property from companies appropriating their data through unlicensed scraping. NTA would also ensure its agreements with members are non-exclusive, so that members may partner with other LLM companies if necessary. The NTA would be led by a steering committee comprised of people working in journalism, charged with intentionally representing the interests of larger news conglomerates, smaller independent organizations, and freelancers. The exact charter of the committee (term limits, voting mechanisms, accountability) would be decided cooperatively by NTA's founding members and revisited regularly. The NTA would also commit to transparency and accountability as to its own funding.

Education. Lastly, the NTA is designed to champion not only the core activities of creating and maintaining an LLM and journalism-specific applications, but also educational and community-building work around AI and journalism. This includes connecting members to each other to commiserate on common challenges and answer questions, and stewarding resources for the general public on how LLMs and AI work.

6. Discussion · 讨论
English

We have mapped journalists' needs as a design space for a participatory LLM, and presented the result of our co-design: a proposal for a journalism-specific LLM collective. Here, we draw out the implications for this moment in journalism and AI, and for FAccT.

6.1 On the limits of relying on commercial foundation models for journalism. Our findings show that journalists are not uninterested in LLMs' capabilities; they are instead wary specifically of relying too much on commercial LLMs like ChatGPT. Past experiences of unpredictability from big tech partners, a sense of unfairness, and a desire for greater control over monetization and newsroom-facing functionality all disincline some newsrooms from relying too heavily on corporate LLMs. Even newsrooms who do opt to partner with AI companies expressed ambivalence. P13, whose organization receives money and enterprise accounts from OpenAI, nevertheless said her organization is "trying to not get them hooked" on the tool—invoking the same language used for addiction. Journalists' ambivalence mirrors what Higgins-Dobney noted in 2021—that technology imposed "from the outside" can lead its intended users towards alienation and disempowerment.

Ultimately, our investigation points to the overall value of trusted alternatives to dominant commercial LLMs. A participatory LLM initiative will give newsrooms more control over how their data is used and monetized, power over what functionality is prioritized, and assurance that they are not subject to the capricious decisions of an external partner. Our findings suggest there are other salient factors for newsrooms choosing which LLM to use, beyond quantifiable task performance: trust, predictability, financial remuneration and a sense of justice. There is also the possibility that a journalist-controlled LLM, fine-tuned specifically for journalist-defined tasks and evaluated holistically by journalists, may actually outperform out-of-the-box LLMs on the functions that matter. Following Suresh et al., this finding invites investigation in other fields being rapidly mediated by foundation models, like finance and healthcare.

6.2 Applying participatory methods to LLMs. Our contributions are also methodological: we illustrate a worked example of Suresh et al.'s call for applying participatory methods to LLMs. Our approach anchored with a partner organization using PAR, interviewed people from a range of roles, and co-designed a resulting sociotechnical system with them. By allowing participants more ownership over the process, we moved toward what Delgado et al. and Suresh et al. describe as more meaningful forms of participation. Our approach also allowed us to take a technical topic (LLMs) and engage with domain experts from a range of levels of familiarity with AI, to ensure our focus remained on real-world constraints rather than technical imaginaries.

Allowing participants more ownership over the co-design process helped us realize more holistic and fundamental desiderata, upstream of performance on defined tasks. Participants were very concerned with organizational arrangements around the LLM proposal: how participation should be structured, on what terms, and to produce what value. From this, we argue participatory approaches toward LLMs provide three core benefits: (i) the ability to conduct holistic and highly contextual evaluations, incentivized by giving domain experts an ownership stake; (ii) improved coverage of diverse perspectives via responsible data sharing; and (iii) education and capacity-building. Each of these goals may seem orthogonal to traditional performance metrics, but our work shows they are essential to realize the full potential of these systems—and achievable most clearly through participatory approaches.

6.3 Limitations. This study provides the basis for an ongoing stream of work, in which we follow community interest toward developing a working prototype. However, our work was also limited; our investigation was qualitative and did not make use of technical artifacts (such as a working prototype of a system) to which participants could respond. Complementary work could explore alternative elicitation and design methods to better understand users' needs in practice.

7. Conclusion · 结论
English

In a moment when AI companies have ingested vast amounts of news data, and now are being sued by—or are partnering with—newsrooms, the news industry has become an important site for understanding the uses and impacts of LLMs in the workplace. In this work, we drew on participatory methods to holistically understand the news industry as a design space, and explore the potential of a journalist-led and -controlled LLM. Our 20 interviews with news practitioners reveal enabling and constraining conditions for newsrooms to collaborate on co-designing, governing, and maintaining their own LLM. We characterize the key macro, meso, and micro tensions animating newsroom decision-making about AI, and present a co-designed specification for a participatory LLM that takes these tensions into account: a brief for what we call the Newsroom Tooling Alliance. Finally, we argue that today's commercial foundation models are ill-suited to the needs of modern journalism; and that participatory approaches to LLMs are needed to complement traditional evaluation metrics with the organizational and humanistic desiderata to meet real-world needs.

• 由各成员组织代表组成的指导委员会将长期探索其他收入来源。

Key Takeaways · 核心发现
Finding 01

新闻业参与式LLM面临宏观（市场结构）、中观（组织间竞争）和微观（个体工作）三重张力，需要在设计中系统回应。商业LLM对新闻业天然不适合——不是因为性能不足，而是因为控制权、可预测性、公平补偿和自主性等组织性需求无法通过商业模式满足。

Finding 02

记者对商业LLM（如ChatGPT）持矛盾态度：既看到其潜力，又担忧数据被侵占、缺乏控制权和公平补偿。参与式设计虚构（design fiction）是探索参与式AI价值主张的有效工具——NTA原型让记者得以具体评估组织性约束。

Finding 03

协同设计成果NTA（新闻编辑室工具联盟）提出由记者控制的数据池、用途投票、按成本计费、指导委员会治理等组织架构。真正的参与式AI设计必须给予参与者"所有权"而非只有"快乐的对话"。

Finding 04

参与式方法为LLM提供三个核心优势：整体化情境评估、多样化视角覆盖、教育与能力建设——超越传统性能指标。PAR+访谈+设计虚构的组合使LLM这一技术性话题能被不同AI熟悉程度的领域专家所参与。
